VU Robotics

Robots are about to become part of our daily lives, and that change is happening here and now. At VU Amsterdam, over 50 researchers develop robots that support a society in which people and technology work side by side.

After generative AI and today's agentic AI, the next step is physical AI: Embodied intelligence, better known as robots. At Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, we develop robots for a sustainable, healthy and resilient society. We bring together the broadest and most innovative robotics ecosystem in the Netherlands, from tiny swarm robots to humanoids and across every domain of daily life.

How robots will change our daily lives

Soon, robots deliver your parcels, tend vegetable gardens and pick ripe fruits in greenhouses. They give you personal advice in shops, serve you in restaurants, help you cross the street and drive you home. They lay paving stones, move goods in distribution centres and help keep crowds safe. To get there, robots must find their way in nature, cities and our homes, and understand what you need. Our researchers develop the knowledge and technology that make this possible.

Robots for society

Robots can support people in many areas of society. Social robots help patients, children and carers, autonomous robots take over dangerous work in disaster zones and creative robots perform alongside dancers, artists and actors.

Social robots for healthcare and wellbeing

Our healthcare system is under pressure, with more people needing care and fewer hands to provide it. Social robots can lighten that load, by supporting caregivers today and by helping to prevent the need for care tomorrow.

For people with dementia in care homes, for example, Koen Hindriks and his team develop robots that tell stories. The robots start conversations with patients and their families, and keep them company when carers have little time. Robots can also help you stay healthy, in body and mind. To keep you fit, Filip Ilievski built StretchBot, a robot coach that guides you through stretching exercises and adapts them to your abilities, for example by offering an easier variation when a movement is too difficult. For your mental wellbeing, Hindriks and his team develop conversational AI that helps you reflect on your feelings or change unhealthy habits.

Children are at the heart of the work of Mike Ligthart and his team. In hospital, their Dream Robot uses medical hypnosis to ease anxiety and pain in young patients during treatment. At home and at school, their Robot Bookworm motivates children to read by talking with them about the books they love. They design these robots together with the children, patients and carers who use them.

Medical students can practise conversations with patients thanks to Jiahuan Pei, who develops conversational AI that responds with empathy.

Autonomous robots for disaster zones and extreme environments

Some places are too dangerous or remote for people, such as disaster zones, the deep sea and outer space. Robots that work there cannot rely only on preprogrammed software, and therefore must learn to adapt on their own. That is exactly what Guszti Eiben and his team teach them. Their robots learn from their surroundings and get better with every generation. In the future, such autonomous robots could search for victims after floods and avalanches, monitor forests and farmland or clean sewers, so people no longer have to risk their lives.

Creative robots for art, dance and theatre

Robots can move, express and surprise, which makes them promising creative partners. In dance and the performing arts, Kim Baraka explores how robots and human dancers can move and create together. Robots can also become a creative tool in the hands of artists. Mauricio Verano Merino makes this possible for artists without coding experience, who program small robots and drones to create art and music. In one of our courses, students even bring their robots to the stage in a live theatre performance.

How robots think, learn, and work together

Every robot that cares, rescues or creates has to learn its skills somehow. Our robots learn from people and by practice, respond to brain signals, reason with knowledge, evolve over generations and cooperate as a swarm.

Robots that learn from people and practice

The most direct way for a robot to learn is from the people around it. Instead of writing code for every step, you show the robot what to do, correct its mistakes or guide it by hand. Kim Baraka develops robots that learn from human feedback. Show them a task once, and they learn faster and behave more safely.

When robots and people work side by side, they need to understand each other. Catholijn Jonker studies how people and AI can work together as one team, in which each does what they do best. A robot in such a team knows when to take over a task, when to ask for help and how to explain its choices, so you can trust its actions.

Once a robot has learned from you, it can keep improving on its own. Kevin Luck's robots learn by trial and error, mastering real world skills from folding cloth to automating routine laboratory work. They also apply what they learn to situations they have never seen before.

Steering robots with your mind

For people with limited mobility, steering a robot just by thinking could help them regain their independence. Maryam Alimardani develops brain-computer interfaces that turn your brain signals into robot actions. Wearing a cap with sensors, you can move a robot arm just by imagining the movement. The same technology lets robots notice when your attention drops or your workload rises, and adapt accordingly.

Smarter robots through knowledge and reasoning

To pick a ripe banana, a robot must know what 'ripe' means. Ilaria Tiddi gives robots that knowledge through knowledge graphs; networks of facts such as 'a banana turns yellow when it ripens'. However, most robots learn by spotting patterns in thousands of examples. Emile van Krieken adds common sense in the form of logical rules, so robots need far fewer examples. It is the combination of knowledge and reasoning that makes robots truly smart. They need fewer examples, make fewer mistakes and can explain why they act the way they do, which makes them easier to trust.

Evolutionary robots that design themselves

What if robots could evolve, just like living creatures? Guszti Eiben and his team see evolution as a way to develop embodied intelligence, a vision they set out in Nature Machine Intelligence. They use algorithms inspired by natural selection to develop both the bodies and the brains of robots, built from modular blocks so that each generation can take a different shape. In 2016, this led to world's first robot baby, a new robot that inherited its body and brain from two robot parents.

Moreover, this team's robots can even learn by watching how animals move, and can master a new movement within 15 minutes. They also learn several skills at once, as published in Nature Communications. Karine Miras explores how the environment shapes evolving robots, and how growth and development can be built into their digital DNA, just as in living organisms. When their surroundings change, evolving robots must keep learning. Anil Yaman gives them that ability by building artificial brains that keep adapting throughout their lifetime, just like ours.

Robot swarms that act as one

Searching for survivors, keeping large crowds safe or monitoring farmland is often too much for a single robot, but a swarm can do it together. Like a flock of birds, a swarm has no leader, yet moves as one. Our researchers apply that principle to robots, for example to drones that fly as a flock in confined spaces, as published in Science Robotics.

Yara Khaluf and Eliseo Ferrante study how simple interactions between robots, such as keeping a safe distance or following a neighbour, create this kind of group behaviour without any central control. Khaluf works with small wheeled robots, palm-sized drones and even a robotic fish among real fish, which learn to avoid it over repeated encounters. Ferrante and his team focus on drone swarms that decide together and fly efficiently. Because no single robot is in charge, a swarm keeps going even when one of its members fails.

Reliable and energy-efficient software

A rescue robot that runs out of battery halfway through a mission, or freezes when a sensor fails, puts lives at risk. Behind every reliable robot is reliable software. Ilias Gerostathopoulos builds robots that adjust their own software while running, so they keep performing when the unexpected happens, for example by switching to another route when a sensor fails. Ivano Malavolta and his team develop software engineering methods for developing and designing energy-efficient robotics software. Using generative AI, they design energy saving software, so battery-powered robots such as rovers can work longer on a single charge, without compromising performance or safety.

Our robots

Together, our labs form the broadest and most innovative robotics ecosystem in the Netherlands, with more than a dozen different types of robots, from tiny drones to humanoids. Our researchers use these robots, or build their own from scratch, to create applications for everyday life. The overview below shows the most important ones.

  • Humanoids: Pepper, the expressive robot head Furhat and our own Dream Robot and Robot Bookworm
  • Robot arms: The Franka Emika Panda, UFactory UARM swift pro
  • Wheeled mobile robots: LoCoBot, TurtleBot, Leo Rover, Lego Mindstorms EV3 and our own Neville and prize winner VU Rover
  • Swarm robots: Thymio robots on land, Crazyflie drones in the air and our robotic fish in the water
  • Modular evolutionary robots: Our Ariel modular robots, snake robots, the robot baby and a Robot Zoo that contains robots from the ARE project, the SPEAR project and the Symbrion project.

Study robotics at VU Amsterdam

Would you like to build robots yourself? In our education programmes, you learn robotics hands on, from the very researchers behind this work.

The Autonomous Systems Engineering lab

Robots are only safe when their builders know how to design, build and test them. That is exactly what Natalia Silvis-Cividjian teaches. In her lab, you work with more than 25 miniature autonomous cars and rovers, 15 smart homes, one railway management system and a model of an insulin pump all built by the Beta Electronical and mechanical groups, and learn to find errors before they can cause harm. In 2024, one of these cars even won the annual intelligent car race competition organized by NXP. student race. Her research also feeds into the lab, for example on how autonomous cars navigate safely based on computer vision and AI or how to safely control a complex machine like a linear accelerator used for radiation therapy. Several of the courses below take place in this lab.

Education programmes

Bachelor's and Master's Artificial Intelligence

In the Bachelor's Artificial Intelligence, you discover in Project Collective Intelligence how simple, locally interacting agents create intelligent group behaviour, such as flocking and collective decisions.

In the Master's Artificial Intelligence, you work with algorithms inspired by natural evolution in Evolutionary Computing, including evolving the bodies and brains of robots. Learning Machines teaches you to design robots that sense their environment and learn to solve tasks, in simulation and with real robots. In Human Interactive Agent Learning , you build robots that learn from human demonstrations, feedback and corrections. Socially Intelligent Robotics lets you give a robot social skills, such as recognising faces, speech and gestures, and test them in a live theatre performance. Finally, Knowledge Graphs and Semantic Technologies shows you how to represent knowledge as graphs and reason with it. Evolutionary Computing is also part of the Master's Computer Science and six other Master's programmes, and Learning Machines is also part of the Master's Business Analytics.

Bachelor's and Master's Computer Science

In the Bachelor's Computer Science, you design and build smart systems that help people, such as a speech controlled wheelchair, in Smart Systems Engineering.

In the Master's Computer Science, you build software that adapts itself while running, to cope with faults and uncertainty, in Fundamentals of Adaptive Software. Software Testing teaches you to test software, embedded in smart homes and self-driving cars, in a professional and responsible way. In Project Systems Testing, you build and test a prototype of a safety critical embedded system (like a railway management system and an insulin pump) in a team. Both testing courses are also part of the Master's Computer Security.
